Man, you know that feeling when your internet just... *works*? No buffering, no dropped calls during a critical sprint meeting? It’s almost mythical here sometimes. So when I saw the news about three ipNX executives getting recognized among Nigeria’s 50 most influential figures in the digital economy, my first thought wasn't about the glamour. It was, "Okay, cool, but what does this mean for the guy trying to deploy a new feature at 2 AM from a small office in Gbagada?"

The article gives props to Ejovi Aror, Bimpe Olaleye, and Olusola Teniola for their contributions, especially around expanding broadband and promoting digital inclusion. Look, credit where it's due: ipNX bringing out their FOS Xtreme Broadband back in 2020 was a big deal for those who could access it. Good speeds are a game-changer. But when you’re building solutions, when you're a startup trying to serve users across the country, "expanding broadband" sometimes feels like laying gold-plated fibre in Ikoyi while the rest of us are still figuring out how to afford stable 4G data packs in Onitsha.

![A person working on a laptop with lines of code visible](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1517694712282-14f494bc6f0e?q=80&w=1200&auto=format&fit=crop)

### The Realities of "Digital Infrastructure"

Ejovi Aror talked about the "collective efforts of ipNX employees" building digital infrastructure. And yeah, it is a collective effort to keep these networks running. But from a developer's standpoint, "digital infrastructure" isn't just fibre lines; it's also the reliability, the cost, and the reach. How many times have I heard a colleague in Akure or Owerri complain about speeds dipping just when they need to push a crucial update? Or how much it costs to keep a small team connected all day? The *sapa* struggle is real, and data costs are a big part of it. We need infrastructure that isn’t just fast, but resilient against our inconsistent power supply and genuinely affordable for the average Nigerian, not just big businesses.

### The Myth of Collaboration (or My Skepticism)

Olusola Teniola mentioned "strong collaboration between industry, government, and other stakeholders" as critical for Nigeria's digital future. On paper, it sounds fantastic. In practice? Sometimes it feels like the big players and the government are having conversations, and then the rest of us – the bootstrapped startups, the indie developers, the tech hubs outside Lagos – get the memo much later, if at all.

What kind of collaboration are we talking about? Is it about creating policies that make it easier for smaller ISPs to compete and expand to underserved areas? Is it about reducing the right-of-way costs that make fibre deployment so expensive? Or is it about creating accessible, affordable developer programs that leverage this "expanded broadband" to build localized solutions, beyond just the usual fintech buzz?

![A busy street scene in Nigeria, showing various aspects of daily life](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1550005810-ca9161a0215a?q=80&w=1200&auto=format&fit=crop)

I want to see the impact of this "influence" not just in recognition awards, but in tangible improvements. I want to see my junior dev in Jos seamlessly collaborating on a cloud-based project without worrying about their connection dropping or draining their credit. I want to see small businesses in Calabar leveraging robust broadband to sell their wares globally, not just locally. That's the real measure of "influence" for me.

This isn't to diminish their achievement. Getting recognized at that level is no small feat in our chaotic ecosystem. But as builders, as people who thrive on execution and tangible results, we're always looking past the headlines. We're asking: what's the next API call? How does this improve the user experience for everyone, not just a select few? The "no gree for anybody" mindset applies here too – we shouldn't settle for recognition alone; we should push for real, widespread impact.

![A line graph showing an upward trend, suggesting growth or success](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1551288049-bebda4e38f71?q=80&w=1200&auto=format&fit=crop)

The digital economy isn't just about the top 50; it's about the millions of Nigerians trying to connect, learn, and build. And for that, we need the "influence" to translate into infrastructure that serves everyone.

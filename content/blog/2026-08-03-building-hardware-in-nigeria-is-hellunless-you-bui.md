If you’ve ever tried to source a specific microcontroller in Nigeria or solder a custom PCB without power dropping mid-joint, you know hardware here is an absolute extreme sport. 

For the past decade, 95% of our tech ecosystem went all-in on software. We built payment gateways, delivery apps, and savings platforms. Toss a stone in any tech workspace from Gbagada to Akure, and you’ll hit three React developers and a Flutter guy. But try finding an embedded systems engineer who understands real-time operating systems (RTOS), C++, flight control loops, or sensor fusion, and you’ll hit a brick wall.

Which is why Terra Industries’ recent move caught my attention.

![Developer writing code](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1517694712282-14f494bc6f0e?q=80&w=1200&auto=format&fit=crop)

## You Can’t Import Your Way Out of a Engineering Void

Terra raised $34 million earlier this year from heavy hitters like 8VC and Lux Capital. They run a 15,000-square-foot drone factory in Abuja and protect billions of dollars in infrastructure across Africa. That’s massive. But money doesn't write C code, nor does it magically tune motor controllers for 40°C heat and harmattan dust.

In the defense and autonomous hardware space, importing tech gets real old, real fast. If a motor ESC blows out or an optical flow sensor acts up in the field, you can’t wait six weeks for a replacement shipment to clear customs. You need developers on the ground who actually know how to debug the board, modify the firmware, and deploy a fix.

Instead of crying about the lack of talent, Terra is partnering with Miva Open University to build actual drone and robotics labs starting in Abuja. They’re giving students access to physical gear—designing, assembling, flashing firmware, testing, and flying drones right inside their degree programs.

## Why This Matters for Us as Builders

I love high-level web APIs as much as the next dev, but building for physical hardware forces you to respect the bare metal.

When you're writing code for a drone, there is no "retry on network failure" safety net. If your thread blocks or your memory leak crashes the chip mid-air, a multi-million Naira rig hits the dirt hard. Local engineering talent needs to learn edge computing, computer vision for GPS-denied environments, and hardware-level cybersecurity. 

Seeing Miva offer a degree in Artificial Intelligence while slapping down real hardware labs with Terra shows a massive shift. We need kids sitting in cold rooms in Jos or hustling in lab spaces in Owerri to grow up thinking, *"I can write the flight controller software,"* instead of just *"I can build another fintech clone."*

Terra is also putting money into things like the South-East Maths Olympiad and setting up joint ventures with local military manufacturing bodies (DICON). That’s not corporate charity—it’s survival. If you want to scale a hardware startup in Africa, you have to build the entire pipeline yourself, end to end.

## The Shift From Consuming to Engineering

The House of Reps is currently making noise about a "National Drone Industrialisation Policy." That's fine, but policy documents move at a glacial pace. What moves fast is getting hardware into the hands of 20-year-olds who have the "no gree for anybody" energy and letting them break things until they work.

If we get this right, the next wave of big Nigerian startups won't just be moving money between bank accounts—they’ll be building autonomous systems, smart grid sensors, and edge devices designed specifically for our dusty, chaotic, offline-first realities.

It’s time to put down the web frameworks for a second, grab a soldering iron, and get back to building real-world stuff.

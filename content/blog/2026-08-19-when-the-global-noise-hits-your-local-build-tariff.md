My brain is doing gymnastics this morning. You wake up, check the news, and it’s a whole different world out there, even if you’re just trying to get your sprint tasks done in a small office in Gbagada. I saw headlines about some political upsets in Florida and Alaska – "surprise primary wins."

### The User Experience of Politics

When I see things like a "surprise primary win," my first thought isn't about the specific politicians. It's about data. Or, rather, the *failure* of data. How did all the models and polls miss that? It's a stark reminder for anyone building products: your assumptions about your users, no matter how well-researched, can be completely upended by organic sentiment and unexpected shifts.

Imagine you've poured months into a product, sure you know exactly what your users want, only for them to flock to something else, seemingly overnight. This political news is just that on a grander scale. It tells me that even with all the analytics and sophisticated targeting, there’s an unpredictable human element. You have to be agile, ready to pivot, and constantly listening, not just projecting. We need to build for adaptability, not just rigidity.

### When Tariffs Hit Your Hardware Budget

But then, the news about Canada and the US almost slamming each other with tariffs? *That* actually hits a lot closer to home. When those kinds of trade disputes pop up, my mind immediately jumps to our supply chain.

Think about it: most of the specialized components we use for hardware development, for IoT solutions, for even decent server racks, they’re imported. If tariffs get slapped on, the cost of bringing in everything from microcontrollers for a smart farming project in Akure to the GPUs for some intensive data processing in Lagos skyrockets.

It's not just the big stuff. Even small things, like the cost of a good soldering iron, development boards, or the specific type of sensor we might need for an environmental monitoring system in Port Harcourt, can be affected. Our margins here in Nigeria are already tight. We’re often operating with a "no gree for anybody" mindset, pushing through challenges, but increased import costs? That’s Sapa knocking on the dev team’s door. It means either we swallow the extra cost, potentially slowing down R&D, or we pass it on, making our solutions less accessible to the very people we’re trying to serve.

The "temporary pause" on tariffs is a momentary relief, but the threat itself introduces uncertainty. For us, planning out a product roadmap isn't just about features and sprints; it’s also about anticipating economic headwinds that can randomly emerge from halfway across the world.

### The Nigerian Resilience

This just reinforces how important local sourcing and ingenious workarounds are. We might not be building silicon chips here yet, but the more we can prototype, manufacture, and assemble locally, the less exposed we are to these global economic wobbles. It’s why initiatives pushing local hardware assembly or even just better access to locally-built components are so critical.

Every day, we're building, iterating, and trying to solve real problems here. The world outside might be filled with political drama and trade wars, but on the ground, we're just focused on making our products work, making them useful, and surviving the hustle. It’s a constant reminder that for all the code we write, the real world outside the terminal window has a huge say in our success.

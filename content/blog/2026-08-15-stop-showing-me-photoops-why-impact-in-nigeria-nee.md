If there is one thing that drives me crazy in product engineering, it is vanity metrics. 

You know the drill: an app gets 50,000 signups because someone ran an aggressive ad campaign, but three days later, daily active users drop to zero. The database is clogged with ghost accounts, yet the marketing deck claims wild success. 

For as long as I can remember, the entire Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) and non-profit landscape across Nigeria has been running on the exact same vanity metric playbook. 

A corporate bank goes to a village outside Ibadan, hands out 500 branded bags and water bottles, takes glossy pictures for a calendar, and calls it "empowering 500 households." Did those households actually escape poverty? Did the kids stay in school six months later? Nobody checks. Nobody tracks the state change.

![Tracking real data instead of vanity metrics](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1551288049-bebda4e38f71?q=80&w=1200&auto=format&fit=crop)

### When Civic Tech Acts Like Telemetry

That is why I paused when I saw the news about Katebridge Impact Advisory launching. 

Founded by Karo Omu (who runs Sanitary Aid Initiative) and Temidayo Musa, they are positioning Katebridge as a civic tech and advisory firm focused entirely on accountability, monitoring, and evaluation in the social sector. 

Their whole pitch is simple: stop telling us how much money you spent or how many people walked past your tent. Tell us what actually changed, whether the fix was permanent, and show the receipts with hard data.

As someone who spends his days looking at error logs, telemetry dashboards, and API latency, this sounds familiar. In software, if you don't have proper logging, you have no idea if your system is actually functioning or just quietly throwing 500 errors behind a pretty UI. 

The social impact sector in West Africa has operated without meaningful logs for decades. Billions of Naira get poured into youth unemployment bootcamps, sanitation drives, and healthcare initiatives, but when you look under the hood, there is zero instrumentation.

![Building systems and tools to track long-term results](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1517694712282-14f494bc6f0e?q=80&w=1200&auto=format&fit=crop)

### The Real Engineering Challenge on the Ground

Building data systems for social impact in Nigeria is messy. It is not like writing a script to scrape web traffic. 

If you want to measure whether a sanitation project in an informal settlement or a cold-chain health project in Jos actually works, you have to deal with rough ground realities:
- Spotty mobile network where field agents can't sync online forms.
- Designing offline-first data pipelines that don't lose records when an agent's cheap Android phone runs out of battery.
- Cleaning up noisy, self-reported survey responses from people who just want to tell field workers what they want to hear.

Karo and Temidayo both have years of boots-on-the-ground experience doing this work. Karo has dealt directly with community logistics through Sanitary Aid, and Temidayo has worked across public policy and emergency response. They know that collecting clean data in Nigeria requires understanding human friction, not just sending out a Google Form.

### No More "Vibes-Based" Impact

We are at a point where capital—whether from venture investors, donor agencies, or local corporate budgets—is tighter than ever. Sapa does not spare development budgets either. 

If a company is going to allocate ₦100 million to a community development fund, they shouldn't settle for a 10-page PDF filled with stock photos and fluffy quotes. They should expect a live dashboard that tracks longitudinal outcomes over 12, 24, and 36 months. 

I'm keen to see what tools and frameworks Katebridge builds out of this. If they can force the development sector to ditch the vanity photo-ops and start treating social interventions with the same analytical rigor we use to debug production code, everyone wins.
